## Ahead of time: salt the meat

1. Put the meat on a plate and pat every side thoroughly dry with paper towels.
2. Salt every side with 1% of the meat's weight in salt (10 g per kg), a little less if there's a bone. It looks like a lot, that's normal.
3. Put the meat, on its plate, in the fridge, uncovered, for at least 45 minutes and ideally 12 to 24 hours (in the morning for dinner, or the day before).
4. The meat will first release salty liquid, then reabsorb it: don't wipe it off in the meantime, or you'll remove the salt too. You can flip it after a few hours if you like.
5. Take the meat out of the fridge about 2 hours before cooking.

<Tip>Avoid salting the meat 10 to 40 minutes before cooking: that's when it has released its liquid but not yet reabsorbed it, so the surface is wet. Either salt at least 45 minutes ahead, or right before cooking.</Tip>

<Tip>If your fridge is old or hasn't been cleaned in a while, cover the meat with plastic wrap, otherwise it may pick up a bit of a fridge smell. Make sure air can still circulate (don't tuck the wrap tightly around the edges of the plate, for example).</Tip>

## Sear the meat

1. Turn on the extractor fan: searing over high heat will make smoke.
2. Heat the pan empty, at medium-high power if your stovetop heats well, a bit higher otherwise. It must be a pan that can handle high heat, so no non-stick: cast iron, carbon steel, aluminum…
3. To check whether the pan is hot enough, do the water test: put a few drops of water in the pan. It should form small beads that skate across the surface. If it sizzles and evaporates, the pan isn't hot enough yet.
4. While the pan heats, pat the meat dry again with paper towels: a dry surface gives a better crust.
5. Add the neutral oil. Any other fat that handles high heat works too (but not olive oil or butter).
6. Swirl the pan to coat the whole surface. You don't need much fat, just a thin film: the meat will probably render some fat anyway.
7. If the meat has a fatty edge, start by rendering it: hold the steak upright on that edge with tongs until the fat is golden. Otherwise, any side will do.
8. Lay the meat flat in the pan. Turn the heat up slightly for 30 to 60 seconds to make up for the heat lost when adding the meat, then return to the original setting.
9. Don't touch the meat at first. It will stick to the pan, which is normal: leave it alone.
10. After 1 to 2 minutes, try lifting it gently. If it sticks, it needs more time: don't force it. If it releases, check the surface: if it's light brown, give it a bit longer. You want a deep brown (Maillard brown!).
11. Flip the meat and repeat on every face, including the edges.

<Tip>Ideally, put a weight on the meat while it cooks so its whole surface stays in contact with the pan. There are weights made for this; otherwise, put a plate on the meat with something heavy on top (a pan, a saucepan). Not required, but it helps get an even sear.</Tip>

## Finish with butter

1. Once the whole surface is seared, lower the heat and add the butter to the pan, along with the thyme and/or rosemary for flavor.
2. Finish cooking for 1 to 2 minutes per side, regularly spooning the melted butter over the meat.
3. Keep an eye on the butter: it should foam and turn nutty brown, but not black. If it starts to burn, lower the heat further.
4. Take the meat off the heat and let it rest for 5 to 10 minutes on a plate or rack, under a loosely laid sheet of aluminum foil: the foil keeps it warm, but wrapping it tightly steams the crust soft.

<Tip>Exact timings, both for the sear and the butter finish, depend on the thickness of the meat and the doneness you want. The rarer you want it, the hotter the pan should be at the start: the outside needs to sear quickly without the meat spending too long cooking.</Tip>

<Tip>Not required, but a kitchen thermometer helps hit the right doneness. Insert it into the center of the meat and take it off the heat at about 45°C for blue, 50°C for rare, 55°C for medium-rare: the temperature keeps rising a few degrees while it rests. Beware, a thermometer can be inaccurate or slow to respond: if you don't trust yours, go by the approximate cooking times instead. If the temperature seems stuck even though those times have passed, the thermometer is probably wrong: don't keep cooking, or you'll overcook the meat.</Tip>

## Pan sauce

1. While the meat rests, if the butter has burned (black, acrid smell), pour it out of the pan, or the sauce will be bitter.
2. Deglaze the pan with water or white wine, depending on preference. The browned bits stuck to the bottom will come off easily.
3. Add the juices the meat released while resting.
4. Reduce (and cook off the wine's alcohol) until you get a syrupy sauce to serve alongside the meat.

## Serving

1. If you're slicing the meat before serving, cut it against the grain: it will be more tender.
2. Season with pepper at serving time, if you like (pepper burns over high heat, hence adding it at the end).
3. Serve with the sauce.
